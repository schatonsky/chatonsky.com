Cap table, dilution and returns.

Build a company round by round and watch who owns what as the money comes in. Price a round below the last and it turns red — a down round, where anti-dilution kicks in. Set each round’s dates and share type, then run an exit to see every investor’s proceeds, multiple and IRR.

Beta — this tool is still being refined. The model simplifies in places: liquidation preferences are 1× and rank pari passu, and the option pool is treated as fully granted and exercised at a nil strike. Read How it works before relying on a number, and if something looks wrong, tell me.

Anti-dilution on down rounds

How this tool works

Everything the panel computes, and the vocabulary behind it.

The maths

Each round is priced off the shares already outstanding: price = pre-money ÷ shares before the round, and the new investor’s stake is investment ÷ post-money. Add rounds and the bars redraw the ownership split after every raise.

A new option pool can be created two ways. Pre-money, it comes out of the existing holders before the money lands — the classic “option pool shuffle” that quietly costs founders. Post-money, everyone (the new investor included) is diluted by it. The toggle switches between the two, and each round’s pool is shown as its own hatched slice.

A down round is any round priced below an earlier one — its bar and its round card turn red. That’s when an anti-dilution clause fires, for every investor whose price was higher. Full ratchet resets their price all the way to the new low — the old round and the new price are solved together, so it can gut the founders. Broad-based weighted average only nudges their price, scaled by how small the down round is versus the whole company: new price = old × (A + B) ÷ (A + C) — far gentler. None lets earlier investors dilute like anyone else. Only preferred shares carry anti-dilution — a round you set as ordinary just dilutes like everyone else.

At exit, proceeds run a 1× liquidation waterfall (preferences rank pari passu): non-participating preferred take the greater of their money back or converting to common; participating preferred take their money back and share the rest; ordinary shares just split pro-rata. The option pool here is treated as if fully granted and exercised at a nil strike, so it takes proceeds like common — a simplification (an unallocated reserve wouldn’t). Each cheque’s IRR is then annualised from its round’s date to the exit date: (proceeds ÷ invested) ^ (1 ÷ years) − 1.

Definitions

Pre-money / post-money valuation
Pre-money is what a round agrees the company is worth before the new cash; post-money = pre-money + investment. The investor owns investment ÷ post-money.
Price per share
Post-money valuation ÷ fully-diluted shares. Every share in the same round is bought at this price.
Fully-diluted shares
All shares counted as if every option and convertible were issued — the denominator for ownership %.
Option pool
Shares reserved for future employees. Placed pre-money it dilutes only the existing holders; post-money it dilutes everyone. At exit this tool assumes it’s granted and exercised at a nil strike.
Up round / down round
A round priced above (up) or below (down) the previous round’s share price. Anti-dilution only ever fires on a down round.
Anti-dilution — full ratchet
Reprices earlier shares as if they had paid the new, lower price. Maximum protection for the investor, maximum pain for founders.
Anti-dilution — broad-based weighted average
A partial reprice weighted by the size of the down round against the whole share base. The market standard.
Liquidation preference (1×)
The first claim on exit proceeds — preferred holders get their money back before common sees a cent. Here it is 1× the amount invested.
Non-participating preferred
Takes either its 1× money back or converts to common and shares pro-rata — whichever is larger. No double-dip.
Participating preferred
Takes its 1× money back and then also shares the remaining proceeds pro-rata. The “double-dip.”
Ordinary (common)
No preference — just splits proceeds by ownership. What founders and the option pool hold.
As-converted
Treating preferred as if converted to common. A non-participating holder converts when that beats taking its preference.
Exit multiple
Proceeds ÷ amount invested. A “3×” means you got three times your money back.
IRR
The annualised return that turns the investment into the proceeds over the holding period — the multiple spread across time. It needs a date on both the round and the exit; without both, it can’t be computed.

Things to try

  • 1With the example loaded, flip anti-dilution between None → Weighted average → Full ratchet and watch the founders’ final % — same down round, very different outcomes.
  • 2Set the Series B pre-money lower and lower until its card goes red, and see how much harder full ratchet bites than weighted average.
  • 3On the exit table, switch a round between non-participating, participating and ordinary — then drop the exit value low and watch the preference protect the downside.
  • 4Push the exit date out by a few years. The multiple doesn’t move, but every investor’s IRR falls — the same return, earned more slowly.
